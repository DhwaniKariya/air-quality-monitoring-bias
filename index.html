Whose air is actually being watched?

America's EPA air-quality monitoring network is the ground truth every downstream air-quality model relies on, but it isn't spread evenly. For this project I merged four public datasets across all 3,143 U.S. counties to see who actually gets left off the map, then built a small model to check whether that gap really matters or is just a curiosity.

03 · The racial paradox

The same data tells two opposite stories

Count counties and the most-diverse quartile looks best-monitored. Count people instead, and the least-diverse quartile turns out to be the one left behind. Low-diversity counties tend to be small and rural, so a handful of monitored counties can hide millions of unmonitored residents.

04 · Controlling for both at once

Poverty still predicts monitoring, even for counties the same size

Population size is the obvious confound: bigger counties simply have more regulatory infrastructure of every kind. A logistic regression on log(population), poverty rate, median income and minority % isolates each factor's independent effect. Bars right of the dashed line at 1.0 raise the odds of having a monitor; bars left of it lower the odds.

Predictors of AQI monitor presence: odds ratios

Controlling for county population size

Population is the dominant driver (roughly 3× the odds per 3× the residents). But even holding size constant, higher poverty independently lowers the odds of a monitor, and higher income independently raises it. Minority share, once size is controlled for, has almost no independent effect. The raw pattern in Section 03 was basically a population-density illusion.

05 · Does the bias actually break a model?

Yes, and the usual fixes only patch it up partway

Knowing the sample is skewed isn't the same as knowing it matters, so I trained a Random Forest only on below-median-poverty counties to predict Median AQI from population, race, poverty and income, then tested it on the poorer counties it had never seen. This mirrors, on a small scale, exactly what a national air-quality model does every day.

Both mitigations shave error from 7.73 to 7.57 AQI points (about 2%) and nudge the training sample closer to the true 13.0% poverty rate it should represent, but neither one closes the gap. Reweighting and oversampling can only rearrange data that already exists, they can't manufacture a reading that was never taken. Honestly, I think the real fix here is more monitors, not fancier statistics.

06 · Why it matters

Why I think this actually matters

Air-quality AI systems, whether that's EPA's own tools, academic exposure models, or health-risk websites, are only as fair as the ground-truth network they're built on. Closing this gap is a basic requirement for any AI system that wants to claim it supports fair public-health and urban-planning decisions.

07 · Data & methods

Sources and every figure used above

Four public datasets, joined at the U.S. county level (n = 3,143) via FIPS code:

  • EPA AQS Monitors: all registered U.S. air-quality monitoring sites, filtered to those sampling on/after 2023-01-01.
  • EPA AirData Annual AQI by County (2024): Median AQI, available only for adequately-monitored counties.
  • U.S. Census Bureau, SAIPE (2023): county median household income and poverty rate.
  • U.S. Census Bureau Population Estimates (2023): total population and racial/ethnic composition.
